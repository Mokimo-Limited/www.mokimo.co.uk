Requirements, systems & process design

Getting the problem right before it gets expensive

Very few projects fail because someone couldn't do the engineering. They fail because the thing that got built was the answer to a slightly different question — one that was never written down clearly enough for anyone to notice the difference.

Requirements

A good requirement is testable, owned, and honest about the difference between what the product needs to do and what somebody assumed it would do. Writing them well is unglamorous work that quietly determines how the rest of a project goes: it sets the baseline that the design is judged against, and it is the cheapest possible place to discover that two people meant different things.

We're happy to write a requirement set from scratch, or to go through an existing one and ask the awkward questions — which of these can we actually verify, which are solutions in disguise, and which are there because they were in the last document.

Systems thinking

Most real problems live at the interfaces: between mechanical and electronics, between hardware and firmware, between the product and the person using it at seven in the morning. Systems engineering is the discipline of making those interfaces explicit — deciding where behaviour belongs, what each part is responsible for, and how the whole thing will be verified — before the boundaries are settled by accident.

Process and workflow

The same thinking applies to how an organisation works. We map what actually happens rather than what the process document says, find the bottlenecks and the handoffs where information gets dropped, and design workflows that fit the way the work really flows. Often that means translating between an engineering team and the rest of the business, which tends to be where the friction is.

Standards and compliance

We hold no certifications, and we'd rather say so plainly than imply otherwise. What we will do is read the standard properly — line by line, against your design — and tell you what it demands, what it costs you, and where you have more freedom than you thought. If a standard has landed on your desk and you're not sure what it means for your product, that's a conversation worth having early rather than late.
