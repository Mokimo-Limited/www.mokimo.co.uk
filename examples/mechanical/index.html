Mechanical engineering

Mechanisms, structures, and parts designed to be made

Mechanical design that has to survive contact with the real world: mechanisms that keep moving, structures that carry their load, and parts that can actually be manufactured at the quantity you need them.

Projects

Beyond the obvious

Ask most people what mechanical engineering covers and you'll hear mechanisms, hinges, and linkages. That is part of it, and we're glad to work on it — but the discipline reaches a good deal further, and it tends to be the less obvious parts that decide whether a product survives being used.

Materials and finishes

Choosing a material means balancing cost, weight, manufacturability, thermal behaviour, appearance, and corrosion resistance, usually all at once and usually with something having to give. Science and medical work adds its own constraints on top — biocompatibility, and resistance to solvents, salts, and pH. Surface finish belongs in the same conversation: media blasting, anodising, passivation, brushed metal, and paint all change how a part behaves in service, not only how it looks on a desk.

Sealing, fluidics, and small spaces

Fluidics, pneumatics, and sealing tend to arrive as a set, and they get harder as the space around them shrinks. Ingress protection in a compact assembly often means moving away from fasteners altogether, towards bonded, adhesive, or ultrasonically welded joints — and sometimes towards holding awkward materials like glass and ceramics without cracking them.

Where mechanical meets electronics

Motors, bearings, and gears sit right on the boundary between the two disciplines, and so does any enclosure that has to be built around a board rather than the other way round. That includes multi-board and flex-rigid assemblies, where a little mechanical prototyping to confirm the fit is considerably cheaper than a second spin of the boards.

Drawings and documentation

Engineering drawings for parts and assemblies, including sheet-metal work and GD&T to ASME Y14.5. GD&T has a reputation for being intimidating, but it is the most direct way of controlling the features that genuinely matter to you — and of avoiding paying for precision in the places where it doesn't.

Designing for how it will be made

A design isn't finished until it can be made repeatably, and the right answer changes with quantity. Injection moulding, machining, sheet metal, and 3D printing each come with their own design rules, and a part drawn for one is rarely a good part in another. Moulding in particular becomes viable at lower volumes than people often expect, and opens up geometry the other processes can't reach — but it also commits you to tooling, which is why we would rather prove a moulded design with prototype parts first than discover the problem after the tool is cut.

Quantity brings a second set of questions that are easy to overlook, because they sit around the product rather than inside it: the jigs and fixtures needed to build it, who is doing the assembly, and how tolerances stack up across a batch rather than in a single careful prototype. Tolerance analysis and proper production drawings exist to answer those questions on paper, before the field answers them for you — a part that was fine in prototype and slightly different in volume, or a screw tightened to the wrong torque, will find you eventually.

We're happy to advise on manufacturing routes and suppliers for moulding, machining, and prototyping, and to produce the drawing packs an external fab house needs to build against.
