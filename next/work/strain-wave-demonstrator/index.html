The mechanism you never see

Strain-wave gearing is compact and high-ratio, which makes it a good teaching example — but the mechanism that makes it work, a flexible spline deforming continuously as it meshes with a rigid ring gear, is normally sealed inside a housing where nobody can watch it happen. This demonstrator puts it on display: an open housing and high-contrast components make the flex-spline's motion visible while it runs, and a coaxial dial shows the output rotation against the input shaft in real time. Students watch strain-wave mechanics happen under power rather than working it out from a diagram.

The finished unit is self-contained — motor drive electronics integrated, running a 53.5:1 reduction — and it is one output of a longer-running exploration into strain-wave gearing itself.

Modelled before it was printed

Before committing to a physical prototype, flex-spline deformation was modelled in MATLAB — working through how the wave generator drives continuous elliptical deflection through the spline wall, and how that deflection carries into the meshing behaviour with the circular spline. That modelling shaped decisions on wall thickness and tooth profile well before any material was cut or printed.

Two rollers, then a proper ellipse

The first physical iteration used a simple two-roller wave generator — the approach most hobbyist strain-wave projects reach for. It overstressed the flex-spline and failed almost immediately. Its replacement, a true elliptical generator with thin-section bearings pressed directly onto the ellipse, did much better: the bearing shields fatigued quickly, but the races have held up well since.

From there the work moved to proper strain-wave tooth geometry — simple involute teeth aren't strictly correct for how a flex-spline meshes. That is roughly where the project pushed against FDM's limits: anisotropy and inhomogeneity in printed parts become real problems at fine tooth pitches, and the obvious next step, SLA-printed Iglidur resin, was more than the project's budget could justify at the time.

Where 53.5:1 comes from

The ratio is not a design choice so much as an arithmetic consequence of the geometry. The circular spline has 107 teeth; the flex-spline has two fewer — 105. The wave generator keeps the mesh point travelling around the ring, and each full revolution of the generator advances the mesh by exactly two teeth:

Two teeth on 107 is 2/107 of a revolution per input revolution — 53.5:1, in the opposite direction to the input. The same arithmetic explains why strain-wave ratios come in such odd steps: the ratio is always half the rigid spline's tooth count, so a useful ratio forces an odd number of teeth.

The work also considered whether the flex-spline could be injection moulded in a high-performance polymer such as PEEK. The projected cost and development effort made commercialisation impractical at the time, but the exploration led to university interest — and ultimately to this teaching demonstrator.
