Learning from the first attempt

Synchronised playback is a demanding brief for a small speaker: several devices must render the same audio at the same instant, or the room hears it. An earlier ESP32-and-DAC prototype ran a software crossover and EQ alongside a modified Bluetooth stack, but never achieved reliably synchronised playback. That prototype taught the important lesson: the sync problem is not solved in software alone — it has to be designed into the hardware from the start.

The replacement design starts with a clean PCB layout and a simpler route to robust operation, superseding the first board entirely rather than patching it.

Designed for real operating conditions

Component choices have been reviewed against the conditions the board will actually see, not just the nominal ones: MLCC DC-bias derating is accounted for where capacitors sit across significant voltage, and pi filters on the analogue rails keep switching noise away from the audio path. Small decisions like these are the difference between a board that works on the bench and one that works in a product.

The derating is worth following through, because in the signal path it stops being a footnote and becomes distortion. As a concrete check, model a 10 µF X7R rated 25 V with the signal itself providing the bias — 12 V peak across the part:

At the zero crossings the part holds all of its nominal capacitance; at the polarity peaks, about 71%. In a filter position that capacitance sets the corner frequency, so the corner frequency — and with it the gain — now depends on the instantaneous signal. With the signal at five times the nominal corner frequency (a first-order filter, ~14 dB down), the gain swings from 0.20 at the zero crossings to 0.27 at the peaks — a ~40% gain rise coinciding with every polarity peak:

Because the capacitance dips identically on both polarity peaks, the distortion is odd-order — third harmonic dominant, the “hardened peaks” signature rather than a lopsided waveform. The remedies are mundane but have to be chosen deliberately: a larger package for the same value, a more stable dielectric (C0G/NP0 where the values allow), or film where they don't — selected against the bias the part actually sees, not its rated voltage.

The board and its supporting design decisions were documented so that assembly and bring-up could be verified systematically — every assumption written down somewhere checkable before the hardware was built.

The acoustic half of the system

A Sendspin node also has to sound right on its own, and the driver response is where that starts. The Dayton Audio DSA90-8 was chosen as the full-range driver, and was modelled in its sealed enclosure — the manufacturer's recommended 1.1 litres — from its published Thiele–Small parameters before any hardware was built: the same modelling-first habit as the capacitor derating above, applied to the acoustics.

The alignment comes out just under maximally flat (Qtc ≈ 0.64): −3 dB around 130 Hz, falling at 12 dB/octave below, with no port to tune and no port-induced one-note bass. That is a high corner for a room-bound speaker, and here the DSP earns its keep: a low shelf below the corner buys the bottom octaves back, sized against the DSA90-8's 2.5 mm of linear excursion at the output the room will actually see. Above the midband the model stays deliberately silent — a T–S model does not predict cone breakup — so the treble handling is set from in-enclosure measurement now the hardware exists. Model first, then measure: the EQ is designed against the plot, and verified against the driver.

Where it stands

The boards are built, assembled into the speaker housings, and working. With the hardware proven in the enclosure, the next stage is integration into the wider multi-room system — measured, as ever, against the synchronisation performance the finished product needs.
