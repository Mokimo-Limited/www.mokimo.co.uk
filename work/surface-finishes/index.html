A finish is a functional layer

Every surface on a part is doing engineering work, whether or not it was specified deliberately. The base material decides how loads are carried; the finish decides how that material meets the world — moisture, chemicals, abrasion, mating parts, skin. Left to chance, the surface becomes the first place a design fails: corrosion starts at a scratch, wear starts at a sliding face, and a "cosmetic" coating choice quietly changes how a fastener torques down.

Treating the finish as a design decision from the start — alongside material and tolerance — is usually the difference between parts that age gracefully and parts that degrade visibly.

What the finish is for

Corrosion resistance. Anodising converts aluminium's surface into a controlled oxide layer — far thicker than the metal's natural passivation, and sealable to boot. Powder coating goes further, wrapping conductive parts in a barrier that shrugs off splash, salt and cleaning chemicals. The right choice depends on where the part lives: indoor equipment and coastal installations ask very different questions.

Friction and wear. Where parts slide, the finish is a tribological decision. Hard anodising puts a wear-resistant ceramic-like layer on aluminium; coatings and textures can be selected to raise or lower friction deliberately, and surface texture decides how lubricant is retained on a moving face.

Conductivity and earthing. An oxide layer is an insulator, so anodised parts need masking at contact points where electrical continuity matters — a detail that is cheap to specify on the drawing and expensive to discover during bring-up.

The masked window is the point: the drawing, not the operator's judgement, decides where electrical continuity is preserved.

Appearance and repeatability. Finish also carries the product's character: matte against gloss, natural aluminium against black. The engineering value is in repeatability — specifying the finish so that batch two looks and behaves like batch one.

Specified, not assumed

In practice this means the finish is written into the drawing like any other requirement: the process, the thickness, the masking, the inspection criteria. Suppliers can then quote and verify against something concrete, and the finished part can be checked against the same criteria it was specified with — the same way every other engineering decision on the part is handled.
