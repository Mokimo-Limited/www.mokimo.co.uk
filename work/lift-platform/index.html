Strong, smooth, and safe by hand

Powered actuators are the obvious answer to lifting a heavy load — until they aren't. For this platform the brief called for high force from manual operation: no motors to power, integrate or maintain, and a mechanism an operator can drive smoothly and predictably under full load. That points straight at leadscrews, which turn a comfortable hand-force into a large, controlled lift.

The platform rides on two synchronised leadscrews, so the load stays level through its travel rather than racking to one side. Synchronising two screws that both have to stay engaged with the same load is the interesting part of the problem — the mechanism has to tolerate the small misalignments that every real assembly has.

Why it can't backdrive

The screw geometry is chosen so the drive cannot backdrive: when the operator stops turning, the load stops moving. The test is a comparison of two angles. Unwrap one turn of the thread and it becomes an inclined plane — the pitch-circle circumference as the base, the lead as the rise. The lead angle λ is that ramp's angle; φ = arctan μ is the slope at which the load overcomes friction and slides down. Self-locking is simply λ < φ:

For this platform's Tr10×2 screws — 2 mm lead on a 9 mm pitch circle — the lead angle works out at 4.0°. Greased steel on bronze gives μ ≈ 0.13, a friction angle of 7.4°. The margin is 3.4°, and it is a live margin: vibration and thinning lubricant both erode μ, so the threads are specified and lubricated to keep the friction angle where the calculation assumes it. The same numbers give a raising efficiency near 35% — the accepted price of a screw that will never run away with the load.

What the compliance has to absorb

The compliance is sized the same way: against the worst-case stack of the tolerances it has to swallow. Four contributions dominate the lateral offset seen by each lead nut — the plates' centre distance, the nut bore's concentricity, screw straightness over the engaged length, and the mounting-hole positions. Summed worst-case:

A 1.0 mm worst-case offset over a 20 mm engaged nut length is nearly 3° of angular misalignment — enough to bind a rigid mount solid. The lead-nut mounts are therefore given ±1.5 mm of deliberate float: the stack fits inside the compliance with margin, and the screws find their own alignment instead of fighting it.

Proven under load

The mechanism was machined, assembled and exercised under real load before sign-off — the assembled system reveals what the parts hide, so the assembly is what gets tested. The result is a lift that feels effortless at the handle, stays level through its travel, and holds safely wherever the operator leaves it.
