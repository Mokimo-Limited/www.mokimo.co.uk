One curve is not enough

Motor datasheets tend to give, at best, a single reference speed–torque curve. It is drawn for one supply voltage, one current setpoint, one microstepping configuration — and once any of those move, the real curve moves with them, sometimes far. Designing a drive from the reference plot alone means designing against data that no longer describes the motor you are actually running.

Measure the reaction, not the shaft

The rig's useful idea is to avoid measuring in the rotating frame altogether. A NEMA17 motor sits on a pivoting mount constrained by a load cell, with a bicycle brake disc and caliper providing a controllable load. Torque on the rotor is balanced by an equal reaction on the stator, so measuring the reaction force at a known lever arm gives shaft torque directly — no slip rings, no wireless telemetry, none of the instrumentation that measuring in the rotating frame would otherwise need.

Honest enough to reproduce the datasheet

Built from FDM-printed parts, a hobby-grade strain gauge and an Arduino Nano, the rig reproduced the motor's datasheet curve almost exactly under the same reference conditions — the best evidence that the measurement approach itself is sound. The brake caliper turned out to be a fairly blunt instrument, closer to on/off than proportional — arguably what you'd expect trying to modulate bicycle braking hardware against a NEMA17's torque — but it was enough to deliver useful preliminary results. Motorising the braking system, to run automated sweeps across supply voltage, current and driver, is the planned next step.

The region nobody tests

The real interest behind the rig is a gap most people don't test: how small steppers behave at higher-than-normal supply voltages — a NEMA17 on a 72 V rail, say. Steppers have a reputation for being a poor choice at high speed and on efficiency, but the reputation softens considerably in applications like small robotics, where low duty cycle and low absolute power draw make a modest efficiency hit easy to live with.

Driven by a good sine-wave driver, a stepper isn't so different in principle from a very high pole-count BLDC or AC servo — and the 300–1000 rpm region between the two motor types' classical operating ranges is genuinely useful for robotics: low enough reduction to keep efficiency high, backlash low and noise down, without needing a direct-drive stepper large enough to deliver the torque outright. Almost nobody runs small steppers up there — partly because low-current, high-voltage drivers at that scale are hard to find, and the lack of demand probably doesn't help supply. The rig exists to characterise that region and find out whether there's a good reason nobody uses it, or whether it has simply been overlooked.
